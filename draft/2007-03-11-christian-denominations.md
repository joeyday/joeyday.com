I was chatting with some co-workers a couple weeks ago at a company get-together and the subject of religion came up. I happened to mention I am an evangelical Christian and one of them asked me which denomination I belong to. I explained that I'm a Southern Baptist but that theologically I lean a bit toward Presbyterian or at least Reformed Baptist. Another co-worker who is LDS later followed-up by asking me what it is about a different denomination that would be attractive to me. I didn't get a chance to answer her question because the live entertainment was starting just then, but it's an important enough issue that I want to take a moment and discuss it.

When I was a Latter-day Saint, my conception of the rest of Christendom was that each of the individual Christian denominations claimed a monopoly on truth in the same way that Mormonism does and that they were all vigorously attempting to gain converts from one another and generally bickering back and forth amongst themselves. I admit this may not be the popular or officially sanctioned view within Mormonism, but it is at least a very common one. If it is not taught explicitly from the pulpit in LDS churches, it is at least implied.

However, when I became an Evangelical (or more accurately, as I studied Evangelicalism just before committing myself to it), I came to realize that my picture of the Christian denominations couldn't have been farther from the truth. Baptists consider Presbyterians, Methodists, and Lutherans their brothers and sisters in Christ and each of these denominations would accept into their membership someone who had been baptized in any of the other denominations without requiring re-baptism. <!--WAITING FOR EMAIL FROM JOHN TO CONFIRM THIS-->

Now, I don't want to paint too pretty of a picture, here. There are disagreements to be sure, and a wide spectrum of beliefs.

2 Timothy 2:19 — quotations about church from Christian Beliefs and Basic Christianity

From <cite><a href="http://amazon.com/o/ASIN/0802811892/joeyday">Basic Christianity</a></cite> by John R. W. Stott:

> Even the outward, organizational divisions of the church, regrettable as they are, do not destroy its inward and spiritual unity. This is indissoluble, for it is 'the unity of the spirit' or 'the fellowship of the spirit'. Our common share in him makes us deeply and permanently one. ...

We have also to recognize that not all members of the visible church are necessarily members of the real church of Jesus Christ. Some whose names are inscribed on church roles and registers have never had their names, as Jesus put it, 'written in heaven'. Although this is a fact to which the Bible often refers, yet it is not for us to judge: 'the Lord knows those who are his'. The minister by baptism welcomes into the visible church those who <em>profess</em> faith in Christ. But only God knows those who actually <em>exercise</em> faith, for only God sees the heart. No doubt the two companies largely overlap. They are not, however, identical.

From <cite><a href="http://amazon.com/o/ASIN/0310255996/joeyday">Christian Beliefs: Twenty Basics Every Christian Should Know</a></cite> by Wayne Grudem:

> Because we cannot see the spiritual condition of people's hearts, the true church, in its spiritual reality as the fellowship of all genuine believers, is invisible. Only God can see the condition of people's hearts. As Paul says in 2 Timothy 2:19, "The Lord knows those who are his." Therefore, the "invisible church" is the church as God sees it.

But the church is also visible. While the invisible church is the church as God sees it, the "visible church" is the church as Christians on earth see it. Therefore, the visible church will contain genuine believers as well as others who do not truly believe or follow the claims of Jesus. But in making this distinction, we should not become overly suspicious regarding the status of those who appear to be true believers. Instead, with benevolent judgement, we should consider all to be members of the universal church who appear to be believers from their confession of faith and their pattern of life. ...

In addition, we should also pursue unity---that is, freedom from divisions among true Christians---to the best of our ability. When we do this, we are falling in line with Jesus' prayer in John 17:21 for future believers "that they may all be one." This does not mean that there must be one worldwide church government over all Christians, for unity can be manifested in other ways. It does, however, mean that every true church should try to cooperate and affiliate with other true churches and Christian groups in various appropriate ways from time to time. Churches that tend to be constantly in disagreement with most other churches should prayerfully consider how well they are working toward Christ's goal of unity among believers.
