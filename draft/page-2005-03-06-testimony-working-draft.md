<h3>My testimony, part 2</h3>

<img src="/images/joey-babypic.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="A baby picture of Joey" />

I was born at LDS Hospital in Salt Lake City, Utah, at 8:17am on September 4, 1979, nearly two months premature. I weighed 3 lbs. 11 oz. and was 17 inches long. My dad tells me my head was the size of an orange. He could hold me in one arm, my head in his hand and my tiny body extended down his forearm.

Most Latter-day Saint babies receive their <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naming_and_blessing_children">name and first priesthood blessing</a> in a <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_and_testimony_meeting">fast and testimony meeting</a>, but, given that I was in critical condition in the NICU, my father decided to give me my first blessing then and there. In it, he blessed me that I would live and grow strong. My grandfather also gave me a similar blessing.

Mom and Dad had discussed a few names prior to my birth, but hadn't settled on anything, yet. Because a name is required to give a priesthood blessing, however, my father made a last-minute decision and named me Joseph Nathan Day. Joseph and Nathan are both common names in the Day line, but my dad tells me I'm named principally after my great uncle Joseph Day (who was named after the prophet Joseph Smith) and my great great great grandfather Nathan Staker.

When my mother first heard my name, she said she would've switched it around, preferring Nathan for the first name. At that, they agreed to call me Nathan even though it was my middle name. I did all right for a premie and was released from the hospital after three weeks.

<h4>My baptism and early teenage years</h4>

<img src="/images/joey-ldsbaptism.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="Joey's LDS baptism" />

On September 5, 1987, I was baptized and confirmed a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. I honestly don't remember my baptism very well. I seem to remember some of the preparation that led up to it, like the interview with the bishop and reading pamphlets about what baptism symbolized. The detail I remember best is that my Aunt Debi made me a birthday cake that resembled a gumball machine.

At baptism, Latter-day Saints enter into a covenant with Heavenly Father that they will take upon themselves the name of Jesus Christ and always remember Him and keep His commandments. They also promise to stand as witnesses of God at all times and to assist those in need. In return, they believe God promises the companionship of the Holy Spirit, a remission of sins, and being born again.[8]

I do remember that being baptized was a conscious choice on my part. Yes, it's the thing every eight-year-old Mormon kid does because his/her parents want and encourage him/her to, but I remember wanting to be baptized and feeling as though I had really made the promises I was supposed to me making, and I'm sure I did it because I loved the Lord. I certainly didn't understand any of the doctrinal issues that set Mormonism apart from other faiths. I just knew I wanted God to be pleased with me and my decisions.

At the start of my 7th grade year in 1992, I grew somewhat weary of explaining to people why I went by my middle name, Nathan, and decided to change my preferred name to Joey. I'm not sure my parents ever knew, but part of my motivation for the change also had to do with a keen awareness of my own sinfulness. At thirteen I knew I was a sinner and that I was failing in my attempts to please God. I thought perhaps a name change would help me see myself as a new person and give me a fresh start. I remember at this time making a great effort to repent of my sins and follow the commandments. As human efforts always do, mine ultimately failed, and I continued in my sin.

Perhaps some Mormons understand forgiveness, mercy, and grace better than I did at this point, but I know I certainly didn't understand these concepts at all. All I knew was that I was failing, and all my best efforts at turning from my sin and trying harder were getting me nowhere. I was never really depressed, but I never felt truly forgiven either. Feelings like this followed me to high school.

<h4>High school and mission preparation</h4>

During high school, I was allowed <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Released_time">released time</a> for one class period each day so I could attend an off-campus <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminary#LDS_Youth_seminaries">LDS seminary</a>. To those whose first thought of seminary is of a higher education institution for the training of vocational ministers, this may sound strange, but LDS seminary is a drastically different kind of institution. Latter-day Saints have only a lay ministry, so seminary does not prepare anyone to become professional clergy. It's more like an hour-long daily Bible study class (except, of course, students study all four of the LDS Standard Works). There is a notion of "graduating" from seminary, which I did upon completion of the four year curriculum.

<img src="/images/bookofmormon.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="The Book of Mormon" />

During my senior year in high school I started preparing to serve a mission for the church. During this time my dad was serving as the leader of the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priest_%28Latter_Day_Saints%29">Priest</a> <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_%28Latter_Day_Saints%29">Quorum</a> and the Bishop had challenged us all to read the Book of Mormon in a year with the promise that those who finished would be treated to a buffet dinner. I and several others in the quorum had been dragging our feet, so my dad arranged to have a read-a-thon at our house during the last month of the challenge so we could each spend several hours of uninterrupted time finishing the book. Under these circumstances I read the Book of Mormon in full for the first time in my life. If my memory serves me, I read from Mosiah (about halfway through) all the way to the end in one day at the read-a-thon.

On April 26, 1998, I received my <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriarchal_blessing">patriarchal blessing</a>. I want to be careful as I relate this account, as patriarchal blessings are considered by the Latter-day Saint faithful to be direct and very personal revelation from God and are not to be shared casually with others. I don't want to offend anyone's sensibilities by simply posting my blessing in full here. However, since I no longer consider my blessing to be sacred apart from those sensibilities, I will share it happily with those who request a copy using my [contact form].

Among other things, my patriarchal blessing informs me that I am a member of the tribe of Ephraim. Ironically, it also tells me I will have the spirit of discernment, that I "will be able to know the difference between truth and error," and that I "will have wisdom poured out upon [me] greater than is normal for natural man." In fairness, these promises are said to be contingent upon the recipient's faithfulness in the LDS church and ultimately to God, but I can't help but find these promises interesting in light of the decisions I made later in life.
