<h4>What is church polity?</h4>

Church polity is a subject I've been meaning to study in more depth for some time now. There are three basic forms of church government: <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Episcopal_polity">Episcopal</a>, <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presbyterian_polity">Presbyterian</a>, and <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregational_polity">Congregational</a>.

<h4>Unrelated related issues</h4>

It's interesting to me to see how seemingly unrelated doctrinal positions affect one's view of polity. For instance, dispensational eschatology views the New Testament church as a separate entity from Old Testament Israel, and thus relies on the New Testament alone to inform church government. Covenant or reformed theology, on the other hand, sees the church as the true Israel, and relies on both the Old Testament and the New Testament for polity.

<h4>Bishop, elder, pastor</h4>

One topic in this book that has already generated discussion with a couple of my LDS friends is whether or not bishop, pastor, and elder are the same office or three different offices, and whether there are any additional church offices aside from elder and deacon. It's pretty clear to me that bishop, pastor, and elder are the same office because the words are used interchangeably throughout the New Testament. Witness 1 Peter 5:1-2:

> So I exhort the elders [<em>presbyteros</em>] among you, as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, as well as a partaker in the glory that is going to be revealed: shepherd [<em>poimenos</em>] the flock of God that is among you, exercising oversight [<em>episkopos</em>], not under compulsion, but willingly, as God would have you.
