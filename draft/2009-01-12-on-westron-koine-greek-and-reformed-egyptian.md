I had a couple of thoughts converge for me this week in (I think) an enlightening way. Bear with me while I regurgitate them all and then try to fit them back together.

<h4>Lord of the Rings</h4>

I did a <cite>Lord of the Rings</cite> movie marathon with my sister-in-law, Kathlene, on January 3, Tolkien's eleventy-seventh birthday (were he still living, of course). I had discovered some time last year that the last line of the movie corresponded directly to the last line of the book---the line by Sam Gamgee: "Well, I'm back."---so I pointed this out to Kathlene. "But wait," she said, "if that's the last page of the book, why are there so many more pages there?" Good question. The answer to which, as any fan will know, is that Tolkien was fanatically obsessive about establishing a literary and cultural context for his stories.

The last hundred or so pages of <cite>Lord of the Rings</cite> are comprised of six appendices with a cornucopia of material about the history, genealogy, calendars, languages, and writing of the peoples of Tolkien's fictional world, Middle-earth. I chose a few random passages of this material to read aloud, and together we had a good laugh at just how obsessive Tolkien really was ((I sincerely hope we were laughing with Tolkien and not at him. To be perfectly honest, it's this unparalleled level of detail that makes <cite>Lord of the Rings</cite> so darn fun to read, but at the end of the day Tolkien knew he was writing fiction. I may be wrong (and I'd love to be corrected by someone who knows a little more about Tolkien's personality), but I like to think if you had met him and told him you thought he was a little crazy, he would've smiled and agreed with you.)). He presents much of this material as though it's a true history of a real world, in a few places going so far as to claim that he had translated the whole work from a language called Westron, the Common Speech of Middle-earth in the Third Age.

A linguist by profession, Tolkien constructed whole vocabularies and grammatical structures for at least two of the languages of Middle-earth, the Elvish languages Quenya and Sindarin, along with an alphabet called Tengwar and a set of runes called Cirth. Besides his two Elvish languages, he constructed bits and phrases of several more languages, including the Dwarvish Khuzdul, the language of the Valar (roughly equivalent to angels in Tolkien's legendarium), and the Black Speech of Mordor spoken by Sauron and inscribed on the One Ring. Since Westron and the other languages of men and dwarves (such as Rohirric, Dalish, and Adûnaic) are claimed to have been translated into English (and in some cases certain dialects of Old English), and thus never appear in his books, Tolkien made little to no effort to work out any of these languages. However, he does provide a few of the "real" names of characters whose English names in the book were translated from Westron equivalents, such as Meriadoc Brandybuck's real name, Kalimac Brandagamba, and Peregrin Took's real name, Razanur Tûk.

<h4>Learning Greek</h4>

I'm learning <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koine_Greek">Koine Greek</a> this year from one of the pastors at my church. He's graciously set aside an hour of his time every week to guide me through a textbook he used in seminary, <cite><a href="http://amazon.com/o/ASIN/0805410015/joeyday-20">Essentials of New Testament Greek</a></cite> by Ray Summers and Thomas Sawyer. For Christmas, my lovely wife bought me two different editions of the Greek text of the New Testament, <cite><a href="http://amazon.com/o/ASIN/1598561693/joeyday-20">The Greek New Testament, Fourth Revised Edition</a></cite> by United Bible Societies (with critical apparatus), and <cite><a href="http://amazon.com/o/ASIN/1598562851/joeyday-20">The UBS Greek New Testament: A Reader's Edition</a></cite> (the same Greek text with a running Greek-English dictionary instead of the critical apparatus).

I had a weird déjà vu moment the other day when I sat down to read the 53 pages of introductory material to the UBS4. The editors' explanation of the various manuscripts they used to compile the Greek text reads eerily like many things Tolkien wrote about his fictional history, only ten times geekier. Seriously, in terms of obsession and geekery, Tolkien's got nothin' on the guys who do New Testament textual criticism.

In compiling the Greek text, the editors not only consulted just about every extant Greek manuscript, but they also consulted 1st–5th century translations of the Greek into Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, and Old Church Slavonic.



April 2020: I've just made a few edits to this draft (eleven years after I started it!) and did a little research tonight about the ongoing Book of Mormon Critical Text Project. The main point I think I was trying to make above still stands, that scholars in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints cannot get as nerdy as Tolkien or New Testament text critics when it comes to Reformed Egyptian, but I have to hand it to them, they do get pretty darn nerdy when it comes to extant English manuscripts of the Book of Mormon. Royal Skousen in particular (probably with some helpers?) is working on a six-volume work on text critical analysis of the Book of Mormon manuscripts. Two volumes so far are critical editions of specific manuscripts, one volume lists out all the text variants between all extant manuscripts and publish editions, much like the UBS critical apparatus. It's seriously nerdy stuff, about as nerdy as you can get about the Book of Mormon text.
