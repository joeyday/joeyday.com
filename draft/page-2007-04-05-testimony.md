<h3>My testimony, part 1</h3>

I am a sixth-generation Mormon. Born "of goodly parents,"[1] I feel blessed to have been raised in <a href="http://www.lds.org">The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints</a>. When I was 22, however, I learned for myself that Mormonism, though a respectable religion in its own right, is not God's truth. More importantly, I learned, in the great words of <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Newton">John Newton</a>, two things: "one is, that I am a miserable sinner; and the other, that Christ is an all-sufficient Saviour."[2] The following is a humble attempt to organize the pieces of my story.

<h4>Abraham Day</h4>

<img src="/images/abrahamday.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="Abraham Day III" />

Abraham Day III was born September 24, 1817, in Winhall Bennington, Vermont (his great great grandparents had migrated from England to Connecticut in the 1600's). Abraham's father died when he was about six months old, and Abraham went to work for himself around the age of sixteen. At his prime, he stood six feet tall and had steel gray eyes. He was an expert baseball player and ax man.

While the details are largely unknown, when Abraham was twenty he heard and believed the teachings of Joseph Smith and became a member of the LDS church. While living in Pike County, Iowa, in the summer of 1839, and fell ill with malaria and lay in a coma for two and a half days. He recovered, and is believed to be one of those healed on that great day when Joseph Smith "went from house to house, and from tent to tent, upon the bank of the [Mississippi] river, healing the sick by the power of Israel's God."[3]

After the prophet Joseph was martyred and the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quorum_of_the_Twelve_Apostles">Quorum of the Twelve Apostles</a> took the lead of the church, Abraham was ordained a member of the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventy_%28Latter_Day_Saints%29">Quorum of the Seventy</a> and received his <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endowment_%28Latter_Day_Saints%29">endowment</a> in the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauvoo_Temple">Nauvoo Temple</a> when it was nearly completed.

It's not within my scope here to relate the many noteworthy stories about Abraham giving up tea and alcohol before joining the church, his service in the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mormon_Battalion">Mormon Battalion</a> during the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican-American_War">Mexican-American War</a>, or his multiple terms as mayor of a couple of small Utah towns after trekking across the plains. Suffice it to say, he was, without question, a man of great character and self-control. While living in Springville, Utah, in 1851, Abraham took a second wife, Charlotte. I'm a direct descendant (on my father's side, of course) of one of their children, Eli Azariah Day.[4]

<h4>Jacob and Magdalena Mauss</h4>

<img src="/images/jacobmauss.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="Jacob and Magdalena Mauss" />

Jacob Mauss was born October 20, 1832, in Leidersheid, France. He was a devout Catholic and was married to Magdalena Kelsch on June 1, 1864, by a Catholic priest. He and Magdalena soon moved to America with their six-week old baby girl, Mary. They spent a short time living in New York before settling in Papillion, Nebraska, in 1867, having agreed to homestead 80 acres of government-owned land.

It was here in Nebraska twenty years later that Magdalena had a dream about two men, one tall and one short, walking toward her house wearing long coats and high stiff hats. The next day a set of Mormon Elders showed up on the doorstep looking much like the men in her dream. Two of their children, Mary and Michael, were the first in the family to join the LDS church. Jacob resonated with many of the teachings he had received from the Elders, but refused to be baptized until he could see Salt Lake City and the Mormons for himself in order to make an educated decision.

Jacob traveled to Salt Lake City with one of the Elders in 1889. While he was gone, his wife had another dream. She saw her husband in a large building standing in water and wearing white trousers and a white shirt. She related the dream to her children the next morning, but said there could be no truth in it because Jacob didn't own any white trousers. When Jacob returned, he told his wife he had been baptized in the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_Lake_Tabernacle">Salt Lake Tabernacle</a> in someone else's white clothes. She took the dream as a sign that she, too, should be baptized.

Jacob had also purchased some land while in Salt Lake City and moved his family there in 1891. Their sons all served missions and their children (save one) were all married in the temple. I'm a descendant (on my mother's side) of one of their children, Michael Mauss.[5]

<h4>Nathan and Eliza Staker</h4>

<img src="/images/nathanstaker.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="Nathan Staker" />

Nathan Staker was born November 28, 1801 in Kingston, Ontario, Canada (his father had migrated there from Germany). Nathan was a very religious young man and studied hard to become a Methodist minister. However, after being taught by such prominent Mormon missionaries as <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orson_Pratt">Orson Pratt</a>, <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parley_P._Pratt">Parley P. Pratt</a>, and <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young">Brigham Young</a>, he embraced the teachings of Joseph Smith and left his Methodist ministry.

He soon moved his wife, Jane, and their three children to <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirtland%2C_Ohio">Kirtland, Ohio</a>, to be with the Saints gathered there. From there they traveled with the Saints to <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nauvoo%2C_Illinois">Nauvoo, Illinois</a> (where they received their endowments and were sealed in the temple), <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_West%2C_Missouri">Far West, Missouri</a>, and eventually on to Salt Lake City. However, before he made it to Salt Lake, Nathan lost his wife to small-pox in 1852.

<img src="/images/elizacusworth.png" class="sidebarpic" alt="Eliza Cusworth Burton Staker" />

Eliza Cusworth was born January 19, 1824 in Lockwood, Almondbury, Yorkshire, England. At the coronation of <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_of_the_United_Kingdom">Queen Victoria</a> in 1837, Eliza was a flower girl (among many other small girls who acted in this capacity). She married Joseph Burton on June 8, 1846, and after a few years of marriage they were visited by Mormon missionaries. Joseph bought a copy of the Book of Mormon from them and commenced reading it.

Eliza was uneasy about the whole thing, having heard some stories going around about these Elders, and after retiring to her bed one night she found she could not sleep. As she lay there in the dark thinking and praying about the situation, the room suddenly began to get light. The light increased until the room was as bright as noon day. Then the light disappeared as it had come until the room was dark again. She took this as her sign that Mormonism was true. She and her husband were soon baptized and began saving money and making preparations to relocate to Salt Lake City.

Joseph worked as a carrier, delivering goods from a depot to local stores. While working one day he tried to lift something heavier than he was able, broke a blood vessel, and died shortly afterward. His dying request to Eliza was that she would take their two children to be among the Latter-day Saints in Salt Lake City. This she promptly did, traveling by boat to New York, by rail to <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_Bluffs%2C_Iowa">Council Bluffs, Iowa</a>, and joining the <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Handcart_Company">Martin Handcart Company</a> for the journey from there to Salt Lake.[6] It was there in Salt Lake that she was introduced to Nathan Staker, and they were married February 18, 1857.

Nathan was a charitable man of no small intellect. He taught school in Salt Lake City, much of the time without pay. Nathan and Eliza were faithful Latter-day Saints. They always attended their meetings, paid their tithing, and taught their childeren to be honest in their dealings. I am descended from one of their daughters, Eliza Jane, who married Eli Azariah Day.[7]

<h4>Why the history lesson?</h4>

I include these stories of my ancestors here at the outset of my testimony because they are part of what makes me who I am. These people (among others from the Bible, Book of Mormon, and LDS church history) were my heroes of the faith growing up. Giving up Mormonism also meant leaving behind, to some degree, the cherished heritage these people gave their sweat, blood, and tears to establish for me. This is one of the reasons it was the hardest decision I've ever made and probably will ever make. However, with all due respect to these great forebears, my reasons for leaving Mormonism outweigh my wish to carry on their legacy, which I pray you'll come to understand as I continue to share my story with you.
